## Design Context

### Users
Curious people with no background in electoral systems, arriving cold from a shared link on a
phone, willing to give it about two minutes. They have opinions about cats versus dogs and
nothing to say about quota formulas. They read it like a short article: top to bottom, one
thumb, no instructions. Secondary: a room of people doing it together on their own phones.

### Brand Personality
Plain-spoken, friendly, honest. Civic without being governmental; whimsical without being cute.
Three words: warm, concrete, unhurried. It should feel like a well-made paper ballot and a
patient friend explaining the count, in sentences a ten-year-old can follow.

### Aesthetic Direction
Light theme (read on phones in daylight, shared in group settings). Warm paper and dark ink, no
brand colour: the four party colours are the only colour on the page and they carry every
picture and every coloured word. Young Serif for headings, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next for
everything else. One scrolling page, one sticky button, one idea per section. The ballot looks
like a real ballot: party columns, every name visible, fill-in ovals. Icons come from one set
(Lucide: cat, dog, bird, fish, arrow-down, vote); no hand-drawn art, no emoji. Anti-references:
dashboards, slide decks, civic-tech blue, stat cards, progress indicators, anything that looks
like an app rather than a page.

### Design Principles
1. **Lose first, then see the alternative, then learn why.** The reader votes, watches their
   party come second and get nothing under winner-take-all, sees the same 100 votes counted
   proportionally, and only then gets the mechanism. The electorate is dealt so this always
   happens.
2. **Strip everything that isn't the story.** No eyebrows, labels, progress dots, back buttons,
   status lines, stats tables or fine print. If a word or control can go, it goes. The one
   button carries state in its label ("Vote for Snoopy", "Keep reading", "Next").
3. **Sentences first, pictures sparingly.** Short plain sentences do the explaining; a picture
   appears only where a sentence can't (the rows of twenty, the ranked list, the council, the
   greyed-out voters). Numbers stay out of prose when the picture already shows them. A new
   term gets single quotes the first time: each seat ‘costs’ 20 votes.
4. **Every dot is a person, and the picture never rearranges.** 100 voters, 5 seats, 20 votes a
   seat, literally. A picture is laid out once and then annotated: rows get circled, icons
   appear, losers fade. The reader's own dot is ringed and every dot picture ends with a legend
   that shows that ring in the dot's real state (grey when their party has no one).
5. **Reveal with the reader, not ahead of them.** The page grows downward as the reader taps;
   nothing is removed, so they can always scroll back. A section that needs two beats ends with
   the question in plain text ("So who gets those?") and Next answers it underneath, with the
   picture updating at the same moment. Next waits until they have scrolled to the end.
6. **Motion only when it means something.** Votes counting in, a row being circled to earn a
   seat, the animal appearing when the seat is awarded, losing rows fading. No decorative pops,
   staggers or flourishes.
7. **Colour-code words, don't overdo it.** Party and candidate names take their party's colour
   (the darker `text` shade) only where they are the subject of the sentence. Lists of counts
   and the mechanism paragraphs stay in ink.
8. **Honest mechanics, said plainly.** The neighbors are simulated, the leftover rule is shown
   not hidden, and nothing is saved or sent anywhere.
