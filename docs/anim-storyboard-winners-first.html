Winners first — three ways to order the results

Storyboards, not final art. One running example throughout: Detroit Sports Legends, 1,000 votes, 5 seats, so a seat takes 200 votes (20%). Tigers 34% · Lions 26% · Red Wings 21% · Pistons 19%. Three seats are won outright and two are decided on leftover votes. You voted for Amon-Ra St. Brown (Lions party).

What changes, and the one question it raises
  • A — shipped today. The reasoning runs first and the winners arrive at the end, as a conclusion you've been walked to.
  • B — winners first, explanation intact. Open with the five winners, then show how they got there. Same eight beats, resequenced.
  • C — winners first, no Act 2 animation. As B, but the "who fills the seat" animation is replaced by two sentences.
The real decision is C. Act 2 is the only place the app shows voters inside a party deciding which of their own candidates sits — the thing that makes this an open list rather than a party-ranked one. Cutting it buys about 12 seconds.

Recommendation: B.

Opening with the winners is a clear improvement over A and costs nothing. People who just voted want to know what happened; making them sit through four beats of arithmetic first spends attention before it has been earned. Leading with the outcome also does quiet work the current order can't — five seats in four colors is the proportional result visible in one glance, before a single number appears. "Here's how they were chosen" then reads as an offer rather than a lecture, and every later beat lands as confirmation of something already seen.

C is the one I'd push back on. The two frames it removes are the only place the app shows an open list working — that voters inside a party, not the party, decide which of their candidates gets the seat. Everything else in the flow would be equally true of a closed-list system where the party ranks its own candidates. The alphabetical ballot order was chosen precisely so this frame demonstrates itself: Amon-Ra St. Brown sits first on the ballot and loses to Barry Sanders, second. C keeps the sentence "got the most votes" but deletes the evidence, and a claim without evidence is the thing a skeptical reader discounts first.

If the goal behind C is length, the cheaper cut is within Act 2 rather than of it: drop the separate re-sort step and let the bars grow directly into sorted order. One tap instead of two, about 5 of the 12 seconds, and the ballot-order lesson survives intact.

Worth testing rather than deciding: whether "here's who won" up front makes people stop reading. It's the plausible failure mode of B and C both — curiosity satisfied, attention gone — and it's exactly what the ease question and a short click-through measurement would catch.
