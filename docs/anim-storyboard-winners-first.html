Winners first — three ways to order the results

Storyboards, not final art, but the Act 1 frames are drawn with the app's real geometry: one dot = 10 votes, groups of 20 dots, same row layout and badges as the shipped component. Running example: Detroit Sports Legends, 1,000 votes, 5 seats, so a seat takes 200 votes (20%) and the grid holds 100 dots. Tigers 340 · Lions 260 · Red Wings 210 · Pistons 190. Three seats are won outright and two go to the biggest leftovers. You voted for Amon-Ra St. Brown (Lions party) — the pulsing dot.

The three orderings
  • A — shipped today. Reasoning first, winners at the end as the conclusion.
  • B — winners first. Open with the five winners, then the identical explanation.
  • C — winners first, Act 2 collapsed. As B, but "who fills the seat" becomes two sentences and the personal payoff merges into the same frame.
The decision is C. Act 2 is the only place the app shows voters inside a party choosing which of their own candidates sits — the thing that makes this an open list rather than a party-ranked one.

Recommendation: B.

Opening with the winners is a clear improvement over A and costs nothing. People who just voted want to know what happened; four beats of arithmetic first spends attention before it has been earned. Leading with the outcome also does work the current order can't — five seats in four colors is the proportional result visible in one glance, before a single number appears. "Here's how they were chosen" then reads as an offer rather than a lecture, and every later beat lands as confirmation of something already seen.

C is the one I'd push back on. The frame it removes is the only place the app shows an open list working — that voters inside a party, not the party, decide which of their candidates gets the seat. Everything else in the flow would be equally true of a closed-list system where the party ranks its own candidates. The alphabetical ballot order was chosen precisely so this frame demonstrates itself: Amon-Ra St. Brown sits first on the ballot and loses to Barry Sanders, second. C keeps the claim "got the most votes" and deletes the evidence for it.

If the goal behind C is length, the cheaper cut is inside Act 2 rather than of it: drop the separate re-sort step and let the bars grow directly into sorted order. One tap instead of two, about 5 of the 12 seconds, and the ballot-order lesson survives intact.

Worth testing rather than deciding: whether "here's who won" up front makes people stop reading. It's the plausible failure mode of B and C both — curiosity satisfied, attention gone.
